Comments that live where you made them

LivingLink is a margin-comment layer for interactive documents. A comment remembers the tab, the filter and the state it was made in, and surfaces only there.

Google Docs comments, for documents with state

Most feedback tools treat a page as flat, with notes pinned to pixels and screenshots. Interactive documents have tabs, filters and views, and what a reader sees depends on which are in force. A comment made on the Metrics tab, filtered to North, is about that view, so that is where LivingLink shows it.

State-aware

Where plus what state

A comment's identity is its anchor and its state: tab, region, any axis the document has. Switch views and the margin updates.

Anchored to content

It survives the edit

Anchors follow the words. Reorder, edit or regenerate the page and each comment finds its block again. One that cannot is flagged and waits for a person to place it.

Yours, end to end

No vendor holds it

Sync runs on Yjs, an open-source library, against a server you own. Comments are plain files on your disk.

Where the idea came from

Client work was going out as tabbed, filterable HTML because it organises dense material better than a linear document. Reviewers were still working in Google Docs, where threaded comments sit in the margin. Feedback came back by email, or was copied by hand into the page, where it froze.

LivingLink is the living version of those copied notes. The author called it a doorknob: the part that lets a document-minded reviewer into an interactive page without being told how.

What it refuses

  • It does not build a backend. Storage, sync and identity sit behind a small seam. The default keeps comments in your own browser.
  • It does not hand anchoring to a comment vendor. A vendor whose anchors ignore state would bring back the problem this exists to solve.
  • It does not flatten the page into a document. The interactive page stays interactive, and the comments adapt to it.
New draft

Information is moving out of documents

More of what organisations need people to read is being published as pages: program guides, product specifications, policies, brand systems. A page can be found, linked to, measured and corrected in ways a PDF cannot.

One statement of that view is ThinkPod's argument for moving important information out of PDFs. It is an agency's post and gives no figures, so it is cited here as the view and no more.

What the move leaves behind

A PDF or a Google Doc comes with a way to review it: comments in the margin, markup, a sign-off. A web page has none of that. The people who used to mark up the document are left with screenshots and email.

Pages also have more shape than documents, and they change more often. A page has sections, tabs and filters, and it is corrected in place. Feedback tools made for static pages lose their place when either happens.

LivingLink was built for those two conditions. A comment knows which state of the page it belongs to, and it stays with its content when the page changes.

Who would feel it first

Teams with formal review are the likeliest to miss the document's margin: compliance, accreditation, clinical and policy content, brand approval.

This is a guess. Nobody in those teams has been asked yet.

Three steps to a page people can comment on

One

Process the page

One build command adds LivingLink to an HTML page and writes a copy beside the original. A small adapter tells the layer how that site's sections and filters work.

Two

Send the link

Reviewers open the page, type a name and comment. There are no accounts and nothing to install. The link carries a key, and only people holding it can read or add comments.

Three

Review in state

Comments appear in the view they were made in. Read as doc puts every section in one scroll for a reviewer who prefers a linear read.

What a reviewer sees

  • An invitation in the page, and one field for a name.
  • Comment cards in the right margin, level with what they are about.
  • A count of comments waiting on other sections, so nothing is missed.
  • A plain notice if the page cannot save what they write.

What the author gets

  • Comments that stay with their text when the page is edited or rebuilt.
  • A tray for the few that lose their place, with the nearest matches offered.
  • Every comment exported as Markdown, grouped by section, or copied in a form an assistant can act on.
  • The comments themselves, as files on a server the author runs.

A proof of concept, shown on real pages

The first column was checked on 13 November 2026, in a browser or by the test suite. The second column has not been done.

Working
  • State-aware comments on four different pages: a five-tab explainer, a report with tabs and a region filter, a multi-screen tool, and a brand system site from another project.
  • Anchors that survive edits, and a tray for comments that cannot be placed.
  • Shared review through a keyed link, between two browser tabs on one computer.
  • Comments copied out for an assistant, and its replies pasted back into their threads.
  • Narrow windows. Comments shrink to a column of markers, or to one line that steps through them, and never sit on the page's text.
  • Ninety-three automated tests of the anchoring, the state rule, the local store and the assistant handoff.
Not yet
  • Run on an outside server, or with a second person.
  • Telling the author when a comment arrives.
  • A real phone. The narrow layouts were checked at phone width in a desktop browser.
  • Comments on images, apart from figures that carry a title.
  • Any site without an adapter written for it by hand.

The first real document was a governance framework for community colleges, made with a collaborator who reviews in Google Docs.

LivingLink is not the first to make a comment aware of the state of a page. What the reading did not turn up is another tool that does all of what it does together.

What the reading changed

This project's record said that no other tool anchored comments to state, and that Velt could not. Both statements were too strong.

  • Three of the tools read have comments that know a page's state. Velt scopes them. Power BI and Strategy store the state and restore it.
  • Attaching a comment to the words is an established idea. Its known weakness is landing on similar text somewhere else, the same fault LivingLink fixed for multi-screen pages on 13 November.
  • The nearest group is new. Tools for reviewing a page an assistant made did not appear in the first survey in June.

What still looks distinctive

No tool read offered all five of these together.

  • It is added to an ordinary page with no work by the site's developer.
  • It follows several kinds of state at once, shows a comment only where it belongs, and counts the rest.
  • Its anchors follow the words through a rebuild, and comments that cannot be placed wait in a tray.
  • Review is shared by a link, with no accounts, on a server the author runs.
  • It reads like a document: a margin, and a linear mode for every section in one scroll.

Each of the five exists somewhere. The claim is the combination, and it rests on one afternoon of reading pages.

It does not have to be the only one

LivingLink does not need to be the only tool of its kind, or to own this corner of the market. It needs to be useful to the people who review these pages. A couple of well-chosen connections would do more for that than being first.

What to take from the neighbours

  • Go to the state. In the dashboards, opening a comment takes you to the view it was made in. LivingLink shows where a comment is and cannot yet take you there.
  • Hand off to an assistant. Every tool in the nearest group leads with this. LivingLink now has a first version: comments are copied out for an assistant, and its replies are pasted back into their threads.
  • Keep sharing. The nearest group is local or passes files around. A shared room behind a link is where LivingLink differs from them.
  • Watch the platforms. Whoever makes or hosts the pages can add comments for their own surface, and several have.

What it could connect to

The Google Docs and Notion parts were read from those companies' own documentation on 13 November 2026. Nothing in this section has been built or run.

LivingLink keeps two things for itself: where a comment is anchored, and the margin it appears in. Other platforms can supply what sits around those. The test for any connection is whether it carries a comment's state with it.

Where it fits well

Hearing about it

Slack, Teams or email

A message when a comment lands, linking straight to it in its section. Today the author has to go and look.

Doing the work

A task tracker

A comment becomes a task in Linear, Jira, GitHub Issues or a Notion database, and links back to the paragraph and the view it was about.

Acting on it

An AI assistant

An assistant reads the comments on a page, revises the copy, replies and resolves. The comments stay with their text when the page is rebuilt.

Two more belong on this list. Signing in with Google or Microsoft would put real names where typed ones are now. Writing a finished round of review out as a document would give stakeholders who never open the page a record in the form they expect.

Of these, only Google Docs and Notion have been looked into. The rest are candidates.

Open as a Google Doc

LivingLink already has Read as doc for reviewers who prefer a linear read. Google's documentation points to a stronger form. The page could be written out as a real Google Doc, with one tab for each section and every comment on the sentence it was made about.

Comments made in that Doc could then be read back and placed on the page by the words they quote, because that is how LivingLink anchors already. The reviewer would stay in the tool they know and the page would still hold the record.

  • Can a comment made this way carry the reviewer's name, or only the account that ran the export?
  • What does Google require of an app that asks for access to someone's documents?
  • Where does a filter state go, when a Doc has no place for one?

Notion

Notion documents comments on whole blocks and sends an event when one is added. It cannot anchor a comment to a phrase. That makes it a fair place to track comments as tasks and a weaker place to mirror a review.

Where it does not fit

  • Inside a published Notion page or Google Doc. Neither documents a way for the owner to add a script, so the layer has nowhere to load.
  • Any mirror that drops the state. A comment copied into another tool without its tab and filter is the problem LivingLink was built to solve.

Where the pages come from

Two more connections sit on the other side, with the tools that make the pages. Site builders such as Webflow, Framer and WordPress are where the one-line install would go, if they let an owner add a script. That has not been checked. Page generators could ship the layer inside every page they make.

The two or three that would matter

A couple of connections, done well, would be enough to make LivingLink useful.

  • The assistant. It matches how these pages are made, and it depends on comments surviving a rebuild. A first version exists: a person carries the comments to the assistant and its replies back.
  • Notifications. Small, and they close a gap in the invite that exists today.
  • A trial of the Google Doc. One page, one reviewer, to answer the three questions above.

From one studio's pages to anyone's

Nothing in this section is decided. The project's own record still calls LivingLink a proof of concept.

LivingLink began as a tool for pages made for client work. The same layer now runs on a site from another project with no change to that site. The next question is whether someone else could put it on theirs.

Install

One line, added by the site's owner

Today someone who knows the code writes an adapter for each site. A site owner should be able to add one line and have common layouts recognised.

Review

A round you can run

Invite by link, see who has commented, hear when something arrives, close the round.

Record

Feedback that outlives the round

Comments kept with the state they were made in, so a decision can be traced to the paragraph and the view it was about.

Questions still open

  • Will reviewers comment on a web page as readily as they do in a document?
  • How far can a typed name go before a review needs real sign-in?
  • What should happen to a round of comments when the page it was about is replaced?
  • Is this for people who build pages, or for the people who approve them?
